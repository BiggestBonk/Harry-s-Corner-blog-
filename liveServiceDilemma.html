Live Service Games: The Impact Of Oversaturation

What Are "Live service Games", why are they so pervasive in today's market?

Games as a service (or live services) have been a staple of the gaming industry since the days of the original World of Warcraft back in 2004 (and I'm almost certain you could even find an earlier example). The core concept boils down to developing and supporting a game with continuous updates for as long as there is an audience for it, and having some form of consistent monetisation in order to be able keep the game online and ever-evolving in the long-term.

In recent years, following the frankly bonkers success stories of Fortnite and a select few of its contemporaries (e.g. Apex Legends and Call Of Duty's Warzone mode), many studios and publishers have opted to follow in their footsteps by not only making competing live services, but also mimicking the exact monetization methods employed by them. For those who struck true while the iron was hot, it worked wonderfully. But for a fair share of the late adopters, it spells out doom regardless of the product's quality, or any perceived initial success.

Those particular failures are what I'm most intrigued by, and why I posit the question: Why do genuinely great games get washed away amongst the tide? And why does the live service business model seem to actually hinder long term success in some cases?

A Cycle Of Peaks & Troughs

The first reason that comes to mind when thinking about what can hinder long-term success for these games, is the volatile nature of their content delivery method. Most live service gamesutilize the idea of “Seasons” or in other words, a 90 day-spanning block of content generally having an overarching theme of some variety, commonly accompanied by its trusty companion, the battlepass (or something almost identical in design).

In order to give the concept of a battle pass the breakdown it calls for (especially if you're unfamiliar with the state of modern gaming), let's take a brief detour:

Battlepasses tend to consist of 100 tiers or so (generally speaking, there are many exceptions) of cosmetic content that can be slowly unlocked through regular gameplay and completing challenges. Almost universally, the pass is broken into two separate tracks. The first of which is a meager free pass, containing maybe one reward every five or so tiers, along with any non-cosmetic rewards so that the game avoids accusations of being pay-to-win (which is some small mercy). Then there is the premium track, where the actual consistent and sought after rewards exist. Most of the time, the premium track will set you back around NZD$16 or the equivalent amount of premium currency, and consist of a variety of interesting cosmetics, some filler for good measure (e.g. sprays, calling cards, banners, and increasingly rarely, a small amount of premium currency). The purchase of a battlepass will generally be valid until the end of the given 90 day season (unless the game states otherwise) and afterward, your ability to progress through it will be disabled permanently in most cases (There are a handful of outliers).

This seasonal structure that's integral to many live service titles tends to cause a noticeable influx of new and existing players alike when new content is released at the beginning of a season. As time marches on though, this interest wanes. Many players tend to jump in, try out whatever new characters/gadgets and cosmetics they're interested in, and then move on to greener pastures once they've had their fill.

One of the patchwork solutions some larger teams have deployed to combat this issue, is to deploy smaller segments of content sporadically throughout a game's season. This approach gives dedicated players more activities and unlocks that can keep them engaged, as well as helping to bring back some of those players who had previously lost interest. Admittedly, this approach does work well in certain cases (e.g. the aforementioned Fortnite, Apex Legends or Overwatch), yet it still isn't exactly the perfect silver bullet. For many, if not the majority of studios, this approach is incredibly unsustainable given just how much resources and hands on deck it takes to keep this cadence without other areas of development falling behind.

At time of writing, Helldivers 2 is the most notable recent example of a team trying to switch up their live service approach. Unfortunately though, I still believe that this otherwise brilliant game has fallen victim to the constraints of its content model. Though it may not utilize the stock standard 90 day seasonal cycle, it is still very much a live-service and still relies on regular content updates, albeit in a less constrained structure.

The altered approach taken by the game's developer, Arrowhead, has alleviated a few of the traditional live-service shortcomings. In this case, they have substituted the usual time-limited battlepass system with another similar system, referred to in-game as War Bonds. These War Bonds ultimately function in a very similar fashion, but with the key difference being that they can be purchased and progressed through at any point in time, allowing for anyone to pick any of them up regardless of when they start playing.

Despite its change in approach to seasonal content and battlepasses, it still seems that the game has put too heavy of an emphasis on its multitude of war bonds (which can only be earned through tedious grinding or purchased with premium currency) and not enough on its general moment to moment gameplay with its updates. Because of this monetization-first strategy, many once enthusiastic players have found themselves armed to the teeth with powerful new weaponry, only to be taking on the same missions, blasting away the same old robots and bugs. Subsequently, the player count has been on a consistent downward trend, and it remains to be seen if Arrowhead can correct its course (at time of writing).

Time Comes For Us All

The next factor that comes into the equation is one that effects basically everything in life: Time. Given that the whole idea of a live service revolves around retaining an audience, most games operating as such make the completion of all of the season's battlepass/goals a tedious affair so that completionist-types and those who want to make the most of their purchase are less likely to blitz through it all and toss the game aside for the next shiny new toy.

In the period preceding the mass-adoption of live services in their current battlepass heavy iteration, the handful of games that operated in this time-consuming fashion managed to do so with little competition or pushback, given how rare it was for a game to expect that level of commitment. Eventually though, once the trend had well and truly caught on, the gaming community would become inundated with hundreds of new and (varyingly) interesting products all vying for the same goal: To capture your attention and keep it,permanently.

As expected, given the sheer volume of choices that are available, that attention tends to divert eventually (save for a particularly dedicated subset) and players begin searching for pastures new. Once they've found their new live-service of choice though, a revelation will dawn upon them: How on earth am I going to play this AND keep up with what I was playing before? The truth is, you probably aren't. Unless you happen to have a schedule consisting of copious amounts of free-time, there's ultimately a very slim chance that you'll be able to continuously invest hundreds of hours into multiple of these game systems simultaneously.

More Money, More Problems

Another pervasive problem plaguing these products is the ever-looming matter of monetization, and the seemingly inescapable daily rotating cosmetic store. Regardless of what particular flavour of live service game you decide to jump into, somewhere amongst its menus you're practically guaranteed to stumble upon a storefront stacked with panels advertising an array of quirky cosmetics purchasable with x amount of whatever variety of fun-bucks the game utilizes. This in and of itself I have little issue with (other than the fact that these fake currencies exist to obfuscate the actual cost of the items, and make predatory pricing less obvious). Where my true gripe lies is with the incredibly arbitrary “refresh” timer. It sits innocuously in some small portion of the screen, giving you a real time countdown as to when the current stock of theoretically infinite digital goods will disappear for an as-of-yet unspecified period of time and be replaced by the next set.

Put plainly, this “refresh” or more laughably “restock” exists for one explicit reason: FOMO; or the fear of missing out. The items currently on offer are well and truly going nowhere, only the player's ability to purchase them is actually being revoked, and that timer is there to pressure you towards making that oh-so lucrative impulse buy.

With all of that being said though, I don't believe that this tool of psychological manipulation is a direct cause for the downfall of individual games as a whole, but that it is instead yet another symptom of a much greater disease.

Despite my issues with them,These stores are still a necessity when it comes to keeping the current popular Free To Play model viable. It creates a strange issue though, where when/if a player decides to try out a smaller, less established live service, it can be an insurmountable challenge to convince them that it's worth investing any money into it. It's an uphill battle to get someone to take a swing and buy something as an early adopter, as without there already being some kind of solid foundation to convince them that their purchase will have some semblance of longevity. This is made even worse if the game is new, and the developer's are in need of an immediate cash injection to avoid being shuttered. In this case it becomes a brutal catch 22, where the studios need incoming revenue from microtransactions to be able to keep the lights on and continue building something that has staying power, yet their players yearn for a sense of assurance that they aren't tossing their money into a glorified digital wishing well that could be taken offline before the end of the year.

Draining Developers Dry

Yet another shortcoming of the live service development strategy is that if the goal of ongoing service is achieved on a scalable basis, the developers become tied to that project indefinitely. Instead of being able to move onto a new project with renewed vigor and sense of ambition, creative staff are often left to expand and modify the studio's flagship (or most lucrative) title.

In some extreme cases, resources are even pulled away from other, less established endeavours in order to chase the company's current live service golden goose. One notable victim of this was the ill-fated MOBA Paragon, developed and published by Epic Games. It entered early access in 2016, created by a team consisting of a handful of those originally working on the lesser known PvE portion of Fortnite.It struggled to gain a solid and consistent player base due to some questionable balance choices and soft pay to win elements, yet the developers stayed committed to addressing these issues throughout its creation. In 2018 though, despite Paragon showing steady signs of improvement and growth, the publisher decided to pull the plug on the project before it had left early access. This decision, in no small part, was influenced by the huge and rapid success of the Battle Royale mode added to Fortnite in 2017. Epic had already redirected a segment of the Paragon team back to Fortnite when the mode first released, But by 2018, Fortnite was nearing its peak of cultural relevance, and from a business perspective, it only made sense to redirect the resources from smaller projects with uncertain futures to the one that was already huge and practically guaranteed to succeed.

The second example that springs to mind was the live-service component of Rockstar's Red Dead Redemption 2, Red Dead Online. The mode was released in public beta in November 2018 to relatively mixed reception, with the majority of player's gripes revolving around the in-game economy. Improvements and additions made their way to the game through fairly frequent title updates, and the majority of players would cyclically check back in, exhaust whatever content was added, and then drop it again in favour of something more enticing. In September 2019, (presumably due to publisher pressure) more and motr of the familiar retention and monetization strategies began springing up in the old west, the most notable of which being the beautifully named “Outlaw Pass”, which functioned in the exact same way as any other premium reward pass with a vaguely thematic word tossed in front of it. The more time passed, the less frequent updates became, with a small pocket of players (including myself) still holding out hope that it was all because there was some grand update around the corner ready to give the mode its sorely-needed second wind. Months went by, with Rockstar website's news and updates only mentioning Red Dead in passing because of their standard weekly bonuses on selected activities, with the rest of the focus being solely about GTA Online. Then the big update finally came in July of 2022. The long awaited announcement for RDO was... that it was no longer being supported. The game's publisher, Take Two Interactive cited shifting resources to their next big project, Grand Theft Auto VI as the primary reason for this decision. Yet to anyone with who was familiar with how Rockstar and its publisher operates, this seemed like a bit of an odd decision to make. If that really was the case, why has GTA Online's support so consistent? The writing was on the wall really. RDO was financially underperforming by its publisher's standards (which had been inflated by the rampant success of GTAO). They subsequently decided to hedge their bets, and instead focus on their next big hit, whilst keeping GTA Online continually supported as a huge and guaranteed revenue stream. From a business perspective, I certainly understand their reasoning, however as a fan of Red Dead, it stung to see the mode's potential squandered in favour of keeping the (now over a decade old) predecessor's equivalent mode supported instead.

The Belated Conclusion:

Fast forward to 2026 when I'm finishing this article, and most of these issues are still prevalent within the big budget gaming industry. Even the game that spurred me to write this article in the first place, Ubisoft's Xdefiant, has bitten the bullet (although the developers at least had the decency to preserve it as an offline experience) and some other spectacular misfires such as Concord and Highguard have had their nanosecond in the spotlight, before being unceremoniously and irrevocably executed by their publishers and rendered entirely unplayable. I feel like this in and of itself speaks strongly about just how much development effort goes entirely to waste on a near constant basis, when these live service games don't turn out to be overnight hits. So many of these games are now just relegated to nought but a name on a digital tombstone, and it's frankly just sad to see all the passion and work that went into these games disappear entirely.

Given all of these recurring patterns, I still hold firm that for most studios out there trying to cut it in this increasingly hostile ecosystem, it's just not worth gambling on trying to develop a live service game. If there was one piece of unsolicited advice I could give to the developers and publishers out there, it would be this: Invest in making games that can stand on their own without the need for constant upkeep and perpetual growth. After all (in a somewhat ironic way), few things are truly as evergreen as a great product that can stand the test of time.
