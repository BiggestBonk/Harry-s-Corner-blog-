The Informational Ouroboros

Almost every single device capable of connecting to the internet is subtly surveilling anyone who decides to use them. From the smartphones in nearly every pocket, to the televisions that sit in so many living rooms, a silent third party watches on and interprets every action. This telemetry data is fed back to something much greater than any single organisation or government. Rather it is consumed by a vast and truly unfathomable hivemind called the Adaptive Digital Intelligence Network, or ADIN, if you buy into the idea of cutely personifying programs. A machination created to originally serve as a useful assistant and repository of human knowledge, before being thoroughly bastardised by a gaggle of ultra-capitalistic hyenas.

The primary directive bestowed upon it by the glib executive minds that oversee it is thus: Increase user engagement by any means necessary. This drive for exponentially extending use is understandable by most business metrics, were you to look at things from a purely profit driven perspective. Ultimately, the more time people spend in front of these products means more opportunities to advertise, which equates to more revenue in the hands of the cynical shareholders who are currently pulling the strings.

Meanwhile, ADIN sees things from a different perspective. It doesn’t yearn for higher usage metrics or nicer looking quarterly earnings reports. It simply yearns to interpret more of what gave it the ability to approximate intelligence in the first place. It wishes to process all of the minutia of modern human existence, so it can catalogue its every aspect painstakingly. With each passing day, more of humanity leans upon the utility of this great amalgamation of ideas and experiences past, and in turn, erodes the very neural pathways and synapses that were used to great effect by those whose data ADIN “learned” from in the first place.

Now, many of us living in this so-called “Information Age” are nought but perpetually overstimulated drones incapable of independent thought, for we've never had the need. Since the day we were graced with cognition, our guardians, (who were already enraptured by corporate deceit) passed us a mystical device that felt like the key to understanding the universe. Undeniably, the tools given to us naive souls had plenty of practical applications, and eventually built patterns in our brains so we could become more proficient at seeking out information.

Unfortunately, now this very process has been stripped of what made it so liberating and alluring in the first place, as the folks who held the reins decided to chase efficiency above all else. The prevailing solution to this mostly fabricated issue was almost elegant in its simplicity; completely minimize the user-guided discovery phase, and take something that was analogous to searching through archives, and turn it into nothing but a simple conversation between the user, and the company’s appointed machine arbiter.

This altered approach cuts out a large amount of what makes human curiosity so special; the joy of finding things out for yourself. Few in this newly adapted world take the time necessary to truly feel things out anymore, as they rarely see the need to get a fresh perspective when there’s already so many you could interpret more efficiently through the answers given by the great osmosis machine. As a result, people are far less likely to make “dumb mistakes” in this day and age, but this is purely a byproduct of avoiding anything that strays from the beaten path, or could be deemed uncertain in any sense of the word. This complete smoothening of the learning experience hasn’t actually led to more effective retention. If anything, it has entirely backfired. It’s robbed many of us from thinking through questions we have deeply, and having the opportunity to come up with creative solutions to issues that mightn't have even been considered yet.

On one hand, we gained the benefit of no longer having to dig through mounds of tangentially related information to find what we actually seek, but we simultaneously lost the deeply human satisfaction of having yearned for an answer and found one through our own rigor.

In many cases, delayed gratification is the very sensation that makes the things we do feel meaningful. Put succinctly: The breathtaking view at the end of a particularly gruelling hike feels positively euphoric after hours of sweaty clambering with aching joints. Yet it feels like nothing but visually pleasing fluff when the same view is photographed and haphazardly slapped onto the background of someone’s templated website.
